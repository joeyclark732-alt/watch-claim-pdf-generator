# The Watch Collector's Claim Readiness Checklist

Insurers made it easy to buy a policy and hard to collect on one.

Most carriers will bind coverage without asking for much. Jewelers Mutual, for instance, lets most customers start coverage without submitting an appraisal at all. But an appraisal or equivalent documentation is required when you file a claim, and that is the moment when a thin record costs you real money.

If your collection disappeared tonight, the payout would be limited by what you could prove tomorrow. This checklist is what "provable" looks like.

Free to use, copy, and share.

---

## Per watch: the identification record

- [ ] Brand and full model name
- [ ] Reference number
- [ ] **Serial number** (the single most important field, and the one most often missing)
- [ ] Case material and diameter
- [ ] Movement type and complications
- [ ] Bracelet or strap type, lug width
- [ ] Any replaced or aftermarket parts: bezel insert, crown, hands, dial, crystal

Aftermarket parts matter more than people expect. An undisclosed replacement can reduce a settlement, and an undocumented original part you replaced yourself can too.

---

## Per watch: photographs

Take these deliberately, in decent light, against a plain background. Most people end up with a dozen wrist shots and none of what an adjuster needs.

- [ ] Dial, straight on, full face
- [ ] Caseback, straight on
- [ ] **Serial number, macro** (between lugs, on the caseback, or on the rehaut, depending on brand)
- [ ] Clasp and bracelet, showing any engraving
- [ ] Side profile, showing crown and pushers
- [ ] Movement, if you have a display back or a watchmaker has it open
- [ ] Box, papers, hangtags, and extra links, together in one shot
- [ ] Any distinguishing mark, scratch, or patina

That last one is doing more work than it looks like. Unique identifying marks are what turns "a Submariner" into "*your* Submariner" if one is recovered by police.

---

## Per watch: documents

- [ ] Original purchase receipt, itemized
- [ ] Warranty card or certificate
- [ ] Appraisal, if you have one
- [ ] Service records from every service, including the watchmaker's name
- [ ] Authentication or certificate for vintage and secondary-market pieces
- [ ] Screenshots of the original listing, for private-sale purchases

Full sets are worth documenting explicitly. Watches with original box, papers, and hangtags consistently appraise higher than the same reference without them, and that difference flows directly into what you can claim.

---

## The two-year clock

The industry standard cadence for appraisal refresh is every two years, and it exists because watch values move. Jewelers Mutual triggers its automatic value adjustment specifically when an appraisal is more than two years old at renewal, and applications escalated to underwriting need an appraisal from within the last 24 months.

- [ ] Note the date of every appraisal you hold
- [ ] Flag anything older than 24 months
- [ ] Set a calendar reminder for each piece, staggered rather than all at once

Appraisals typically run somewhere in the $50 to $150 range per watch. Shop around: collectors report meaningful spread between local watchmakers, jewelers, and specialists.

---

## Storage

Documentation that burns in the same house fire as your watches is not documentation.

- [ ] One copy in cloud storage
- [ ] One copy on a drive you keep somewhere other than home
- [ ] Physical originals in a fireproof safe or bank box, not the watch box
- [ ] Someone you trust knows where the record is and how to reach it

---

## Your policy

- [ ] Confirm your homeowners jewelry sublimit. It is typically $1,000 to $5,000, often per occurrence rather than per item.
- [ ] Confirm whether each watch is individually scheduled
- [ ] Confirm whether coverage includes mysterious disappearance, not just theft
- [ ] Confirm whether you are covered while traveling internationally
- [ ] Confirm whether it is agreed value or actual cash value

Agreed value versus actual cash value is the question most people never ask and most regret not asking.

---

## If the worst happens

1. Police report immediately, with serial numbers included
2. Notify your insurer within the window your policy specifies
3. Register the serials with a stolen watch registry
4. Notify the brand's service network, since stolen pieces often surface at service
5. Send your claim file, complete, in one package

---

## The honest summary

Three things matter more than everything else combined: **serial numbers recorded, proof of value attached, appraisals under two years old.** If you only do three things this weekend, do those, for your most valuable pieces first.
